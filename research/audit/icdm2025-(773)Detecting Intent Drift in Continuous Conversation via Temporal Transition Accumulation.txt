Abstract—As large language models (LLMs)-driven conversa-
tional systems have advanced, users have become accustomed to
engaging in standalone, continuous interactions. In such interac-
tions, users may abruptly change their intent across turns. How-
ever, most existing intent detection methods focus on accurately
extracting intents and slots from individual utterances, without
considering the broader conversational dynamics. This makes
them ill-equipped to handle long, evolving conversations where
arbitrary intent drift can occur. To address this challenge, we
define the intent drift detection task in continuous conversations.
We then propose a differentiable method, termed DriftHunter,
that enables neural networks to understand how user intent shifts
as the conversation progresses via dynamically accumulating the
temporal transition across turns. Unlike existing methods, our
proposed method incrementally captures global and local transi-
tion patterns between intents and slots without relying on prior
statistical results. Moreover, our model sequentially accumulates
transition patterns across conversation turns. This allows it to
learn temporal accumulated dynamics, enabling neural network
models to better focus on the most trending user intents during
continuous interaction. Experimental evaluations on real-world
datasets demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms
state-of-the-art baselines in both intent drift detection, intent
identification, and slot-filling downstream tasks. Our case study
analysis reveals that the learned temporal transition patterns
explain the predicted intent drifts.1
Index Terms—Intent drift detection, standalone-continuous

I. I NTRODUCTION
The powerful capabilities of large language models (LLMs)
on natural language understanding and generation [1] enable
question answering (QA) systems [2] and chatbots [3] to
facilitate more natural and human-like interactions. As a
result, users accustomed to continuous single-session conver-
sations [4], and often perceive these systems as genuine human
customer service agents. This perception frequently leads users
to overlook the necessity of manually switching sessions when
engaging with distinct intents.
To provide accurate responses, QA and chatbot systems
utilize various intent identification techniques to discern users’

context. For instance, a user might input ”SFT training” in
a machine learning context, but the chatbot could incorrectly

Prior intent classification work in multi-turn QA systems [6]
employed recurrent neural network (RNN) architectures to
model contextual dependencies between adjacent timesteps,
thereby capturing intent drift patterns. To better leverage
contextual information, some approaches incorporated latent
features from fixed max-length conversation contexts for in-
tent classification [7]. However, these methods fail to fully
address the latent relationships between intents and slots in
conversational contexts, making them struggle with handling
arbitrary intent drifts. More recently, heterogeneous network-
based methods [11] have provided new insights into the
problem of intent drift in continuous conversations. These
approaches improve performance in continuous conversational
contexts by constructing a global heterogeneous label graph
that captures intent-slot transition relationships. Meanwhile,
recent LLM-driven chat systems [1], with their ability to
process long sequences, also show potential in managing
standalone continuous conversations. However, existing meth-
ods [11], [12] disregard the temporal transition information
across conversation turns, leading to performance degradation
in long, continuous conversations with arbitrary intent drifts.
To address this challenge, we propose a differentiable mod-
ule, DriftHunter, that detects the intent drifts via temporal
transition accumulation. This module is designed to capture
the temporal dynamics of past conversational context by dis-
covering and accumulating transition patterns between intents
and slots in user utterances as the conversation progresses.
Through this mechanism, DriftHunter efficiently constructs a
global joint label (with both intent and slot labels) structure
that represents the statistical relationships between intents and
slots. The accumulated transition structure not only captures
sequential patterns but also updates dynamically as new user
utterances are introduced. Therefore, our proposed module
enables neural network models to achieve better performance
across multiple tasks, including intent drift detection, tradi-
tional intent identification, and slot-filling. Unlike existing
methods that require an offline process to construct a global
statistical structure before training, our approach supports end-
to-end training. This enables the model to simultaneously
learn global transitions between intents and slots, and the
sequentially evolving patterns across conversational turns.
Consequently, our approach is highly effective for large-scale
QA systems serving multiple concurrent users. It can function
as an online switching mechanism, dynamically adjusting the
range of context to optimize performance.
We conduct comprehensive experiments on real-world pub-
lic datasets. The experimental results show that our proto-
type system significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines
across intent drift detection, intent identification, and slot-
filling tasks. Applied to existing models, DriftHunter boosts
their average F1 scores by 6.4% in intent drift detection,
4.6% in intent detection, and 7.3% in slot filling. This verifies
that our proposed DriftHunter effectively captures global het-
erogeneous information within user utterances in continuous
conversations. In our case study, analysis of DriftHunter’s
trained parameters showed that the learned patterns accurately
capture ground-truth user intents and reflect intent drift. This
highlights our method’s efficacy in identifying high-quality,
temporally evolving patterns in QA interactions.
Contributions. In summary, the primary contributions of this
paper are as follows:
• To the best of our knowledge, we first formulate the intent
drift detection task. We also discuss its related challenges
in a standalone and continuous conversation scenario.
• We propose a module DriftHunter, designed to end-to-
end accumulate heterogeneous information between intents
and slots as the conversation progresses. This is the first
differentiable method to accumulate the temporal transition
dynamics between intents and slots across different turns.
• We verify that by integrating DriftHunter, neural network
models could achieve better performance on both the intent
drift detection, intent identification, and slot filling tasks.
Furthermore, the accumulated transitions observed in the
case study demonstrate the proposed model’s ability to align
with the ground-truth user intents promptly. This further
indicates that DriftHunter effectively captures the actual
temporal dynamics underlying intent drifts.
II. R ELATED WORKS
Spoken Language Understanding (SLU) [9] is crucial for
dialogue systems, focusing on extracting structural seman-
tic information from user utterances. Key SLU sub-tasks
include intent detection [13], slot filling [14], and domain
classification [15]. Current SLU models commonly employ
an encoder-decoder framework and are broadly categorized
as task-specific or joint models [16]. Joint models utilize a
multi-task framework to address sub-tasks concurrently. Since
joint models can leverage the hidden information between sub-
tasks to achieve better performance on each, we also apply this
framework in our work.
Intent identification, also known as intent classification [13],
aims to determine the user’s intention from their utterance. Slot
filling [14] is a related task concerned with extracting relevant
arguments (slots) pertinent to a detected intent within natural
language utterances. Recognizing the inherent connection be-
tween these tasks, recent research has increasingly explored
joint intent detection and slot filling models [9] under the
multitask learning framework. Since [17], the self-attention
mechanism has proven highly effective within the field of
natural language processing. The attention mechanism has
been widely adopted in intent detection and slot filling [12],
[18] to enhance the semantic representation.
However, most of these methods are designed for single-turn
conversational contexts and struggle to address the complexi-
ties of continuous and multi-turn QA scenarios.
With the advancement of LLM-driven chatbot systems,
users are increasingly accustomed to continuous single-session

conversations [4]. Therefore, more and more researches focus
on the intent identification problem in multi-turn or contin-
uous conversation [19]. The primary consideration for intent
identification in multi-turn conversations is the approach taken
to leverage the contextual information available across the di-
alogue. Pioneering work by Mensio et al. [6] aided subsequent
intent identification by concatenating contexts from adjacent
time steps. Building upon this, Tran et al. [7] applied static,
complete session contexts to further enhance intent classifica-
tion. More recently, Xing et al. [11] discovered the potential
of the relationship between intents and slots to improve intent
identification performance in multi-turn continuous conversa-
tions. Consequently, Zhu et al. [12] employed a statistical
approach to capture the global relationships between intents
and slots, thereby improving the model’s understanding of the
context and leading to further performance gains. However, de-
spite the benefits of leveraging statistical relationships between
intents and slots, the current method experiences performance
degradation when intents drift arbitrarily in continuous and
standalone conversations.
III. P RELIMINARIES
A. Basic Notations
In QA or chatbot systems, users interact by entering short
text messages or utterances, and receive responses that contain
the requested information. Therefore, accurately determining
the user’s intent is the crucial first step in generating suitable
answers. We give the formal definitions for the related nota-
tions by following the previous works [12].
Intent and Slot of User Utterance. Given a user utterance
u, its intent is represented by a class label c, where c belongs
to a predefined set of intent classes C. Intent c reflects the
underlying goal or purpose of u; the slots of u are several
pieces of u that provides specific details related to the user’s
intent. For instance, when the input is “The causes of autism,”
the system identifies the intent as “inquire about illness,”
and extracts the following slots: “illness name: autism” and
“information type: causes.”
Continuous and Standalone Chat System. A continuous and
standalone chat system maintains a persistent session. This
allows for uninterrupted user interaction. Unlike traditional
systems, it does not necessitate manual intent switching for
subsequent utterances. e.g., LLM-driven chatbot systems often
include session switch buttons. However, most users tend to
overlook these. They interact with the system continuously,
treating it like a persistent human.
B. Problem Definition
Intent drift occurs in the following scenario: a user might
initially stick to an intent due to unsuitable chatbot responses
or the need for more relevant information. However, within
the same session, they may later change their intent when a
new requirement arises. We formally define intent drift and
the problem of its detection as follows.
Intent Drift. In a QA system, let U = (u1, u2, u3, . . . , un) be
a sequence of user utterances within a single session. Suppose
u1, u2, u3, ..., uk (k < n − 1) have the intent ck, and uk+1
relates to an intent c′, where c′ ̸= ck, then an intent drift
occurs at the k-th turn of the conversation, denoted as ok = 1
(indicating a drift) and ok = 0 otherwise.
We observe that the intent drift occurs arbitrarily in the
QA systems. As introduced earlier, users accustomed to LLM-
driven chatbots may provide additional information for a prior
intent due to factors such as unsatisfactory responses or the
need for more relevant information. This habit of omitting
manual session switching can lead to arbitrary intent shifts
when new requirements arise. To this end, we provide the
following definition for the intent drift detection problem.
Intent Drift Detection in Continuous Conversation. With
the same setting of intent drift, the intent drift detector in
continuous conversation is a binary classifier. It is defined as
the following function.
gθ(u1, u2, . . . , uk, uk+1) → {0, 1}, (1)
where gθ denotes a model parameterized by θ that predicts the
intent drift at the k-th conversational turn using the context
from u1 to uk. Then, the intent drift detection is a task of
optimizing θ to minimize the following equation.

k=1,2,...,n
L(ok, gθ(u1, u2, . . . , uk, uk+1)), (2)
where L represents any suitable loss function that quantifies
the discrepancy between the predicted label and the ground-
truth ok for the intent drift at the k-th conversational turn.
This problem can be treated as an extension of intent
classification. Detected intent drifts can serve as crucial signals
for chatbot systems, prompting improvements in intent clas-
sification and slot-filling performance. Existing intent classifi-
cation methods statistically capture static, global relationships
between intents and slots, but overlook dynamic temporal
relationships across conversational turns that indicate the intent
drift patterns. Neglecting this temporal aspect introduces a
bias between the overall conversational context and the recent
utterance, posing a significant challenge to accurately predict
intents in a continuous conversation with intent drifts.

V. E XPERIMENTS AND DISCUSSIONS
We aim to address the following research questions (RQ).
• RQ1: Can the proposed DriftHunter enhance the ability of
models to detect intent drift across different scenarios?
• RQ2: What are the specific uses of the four components of
the proposed DriftHunter mechanism?
• RQ3: How sensitive is DriftHunter to different parameters?
• RQ4: How to explain the learned parameters?
A. Datasets
To thoroughly evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we
have selected three distinct datasets that are widely utilized in
the field of intent classification and slot filling:
• Airline Travel Information System (ATIS) [25] dataset is a
collection of spoken queries and responses related to airline
travel, such as flight reservations, flight status, and airport
information. This dataset is not treated as a conversation
dataset, and each sentence has an intent.
• The Simulated-Dialogue dataset (Sim) [26] is an open-
source, multi-turn dialogue dataset encompassing various
application scenarios, including restaurant reservations and
movie ticket purchases. Each dialogue in the dataset is
designed to reflect a single intent.
• Multi-Domain Wizard-of-Oz Dataset (MultiWOZ) [27] is a
widely recognized task-oriented dialogue dataset comprising
over 10,000 annotated conversations across 8 domains. It is
extensively used as a benchmark for dialogue state tracking.
Each sentence conveys a specific intent, and these intents
may change between sentences throughout the dialogue.

utterance, marking any as intent drift if its intent differed from

C. Baselines
• BERT [5] employs a pre-trained BERT encoder fine-tuned
for joint intent detection and slot filling. It formulates
both tasks as token-level and sentence-level classification
problems, respectively, using the shared contextualized rep-
resentations from BERT.
• RNNContextual [6] models contextual information by lever-
aging recurrent neural networks (RNNs) across dialogue
turns. It updates the current utterance representation by in-
corporating the information from previous turns to improve
multi-turn spoken language understanding.
• AGLCF [7] introduces an adaptive global-local context
fusion mechanism. It employs multi-head attention to sepa-
rately model global dialogue-level and local utterance-level
information, and then fuses them to enhance both intent

D. Implementation Details
For our experiments, the maximum number of tokens for
each utterance is 128, and the window size of the previous
utterance is 32. For the comparison tasks, we used Cosine
similarity and Softmax as theSimilarityand the Activation
functions, respectively. We utilize the pretrained bert-based-
uncased model of BERT [21] for embedding, resulting in a
dimensionality (D) of 768. We set λ in Equation 11 to 0.2
for all experiments. The rationale behind this, along with the
settings for other hyperparameters likeα, β and γ, is detailed
in Section V-E3. We adopt AdamW [28] to train the model
with the learning rate of 1e − 5, and we select the best
performance on the test set of each model. All experiments
are conducted on an Nvidia 4090 GPU, and we fix the random
seed to ensure reproduction.

detection, and slot filling. As shown in Table III and IV, the
performance of the version with DriftHunter generally out-
performs the version without DriftHunter across all subtasks
for each baseline. This result indicates that our method has
effectively learned to detect intent drift in real-world scenarios.

models still struggle with understanding intent drift, aligning
with the motivating example discussed earlier.

2) Ablation Study (RQ2): To evaluate the contribution of
each component within our proposed module, we performed
an study of the model across all three datasets. Given that
AGLCF-DH achieved the best intent drift detection perfor-
mance across all datasets, we selected it for this analysis.
This ablation study was conducted by removing two key
components of our DriftHunter module, intent-slot transition
accumulation and global transition incorporation, across the
three datasets. The results are reported in Table VIII. The
results indicate that intent-slot transition accumulation primar-
ily improves recall, whereas global transition incorporation
enhances precision and F1-score. Together, these components
lead to an overall performance gain.
To further investigate the contribution of intent drift de-
tection to overall performance, we tested AGLCF-DH’s per-
formance on intent detection and slot filling after removing
the intent drift detection subtask. As Tables IX and X show,
removing this module led to a noticeable drop in F1-score for
both tasks. These results demonstrate that explicitly modeling
intent drift enhances the model’s understanding of dialogue
dynamics and improves performance.

4) Case Study (RQ4): To further investigate the dynamics
of intent drift and maintenance, we visualized the intent
transition matrix as a heatmap using our model on the SIM
data. To enhance the visualization of differences, we employed
dot similarity asSimilarityand Tanh asActivationin Eq. 10.
The analyzed cases were selected from the SIM test dataset
using AGLCF-DH since it performs the best in intent drift
detection across all datasets.
Figure 4 illustrates that as the user’s intent shifts from “buy
movie tickets (BMT)” to “reserve restaurant (RR)”, there is
an increasing trend in the corresponding cross-turn temporal
transition. This indicates the DriftHunter effectively captures
temporal dependencies between intents. The heatmaps also
demonstrate the model’s ability to learn sequential user be-
haviors, such as planning dinner after buying movie tickets.
Figures 5 illustrates that when user intent remains consistent
across interactions, the DriftHunter module effectively main-
tains high self-transition attention scores while suppressing
irrelevant intent transitions. This indicates that the model
effectively captures the temporal consistency of user behavior
and avoids introducing noise from irrelevant intent paths.
The ability to recognize and reinforce stable intent patterns
highlights the DriftHunter module’s strength in modeling both
dynamic drifts and steady states in multi-turn dialogues.

VI. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we address the prevalent intent drift problem
in QA systems by first formalizing its definition. We then
propose a neural network module, the DriftHunter, to tackle
intent drift detection alongside intent detection and slot filling

is differentiable, it can be incorporated into any related neural
